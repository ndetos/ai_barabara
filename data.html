The central question

Why collect Kenyan data? Open-source datasets already exist.

This is the question every funder should ask — and the one every AI team must be able to answer. The honest answer is: open-source datasets are essential for pre-training, but insufficient for deployment. RDD2022 gave us a running start. It cannot tell us how a pothole looks on a Kenyan urban arterial after the long rains, in the specific lighting and surface conditions KURA engineers actually face.

The core problem is domain shift: a model trained on images from one set of countries learns visual patterns that do not transfer cleanly to another. This is not a theoretical concern — it is measured. A recent study of AI colonoscopy models trained on European data and tested on Egyptian data found a 29% absolute drop in detection accuracy (mAP@50 fell from 0.963–0.991 to 0.702) purely because of morphological and colour differences between populations[citation:3]. The same mechanism applies to road damage: a pothole in Norway (cold climate, studded tyres, different asphalt mix) does not look like a pothole in Nairobi (tropical rainfall, heavy axle loads, different maintenance history).

The RDD2022 benchmark itself was built to address exactly this gap — its authors explicitly note that the earlier single-country datasets (RDD2018, RDD2019) used Japanese road maintenance standards, and that expanding to multiple countries was necessary precisely because "adding data from other countries helps improve the generalizability of models trained for detecting road damage in any country"[citation:11]. But even RDD2022 covers only six countries: Japan, India, the Czech Republic, Norway, China, and the United States. No African country is represented. Kenya's road conditions, climate, and construction standards are absent from the benchmark entirely.

This is not a Kenyan problem alone. A 2025 World Bank study on road quality prediction in Kenya, Liberia, and the Democratic Republic of Congo used geographically-based data splits — training on one country and testing on another — specifically because the authors recognised that models trained on one geography do not generalise reliably to another[citation:5]. And in clinical AI, Oxford researchers have cautioned against deploying "region- and culture-naïve models without thorough prior evaluation", recommending that models be adapted to the specific contexts of their intended deployment[citation:13].

The conclusion is not that open-source data is useless. It is that RDD2022 is the starting point, not the finish line. Our model achieves mAP50 ≈ 0.49 on the RDD2022 subset — a credible baseline. The next step, which only Kenyan imagery can enable, is to fine-tune that baseline on the roads KURA actually manages. A model that has seen 40 km of Kenyan urban roads will outperform one that has only seen Japanese and Norwegian roads, on Kenyan roads.

This is why Phase 1 includes 40–50 km of Kenyan imagery collection with GPS metadata. It is not a nice-to-have. It is the difference between a model that works in a lab and a model that works for KURA.

Note on citations: The domain-shift evidence cited above comes from clinical AI and World Bank road-quality research, not from a road-damage-specific study in Kenya. This is because no peer-reviewed study yet exists that directly measures the performance drop of a road-damage model trained outside Africa and tested in Kenya. The mechanism — visual domain shift — is well-established across computer vision applications. The Kenyan pilot imagery collection is designed to generate exactly this evidence for road damage.
